Dear Editor,

I am submitting my manuscript, "Does Naive Cross-Validation Inflate Software
Energy-Regression Prediction? A Leakage-Aware, Statistically-Gated, Cross-Project
Empirical Study," for consideration as an original research article in Information
and Software Technology.

This manuscript is an empirical software engineering study addressing evaluation
methodology for machine-learning-based prediction tasks in software engineering,
using energy-regression prediction as its object of study. We construct a
cross-project, cross-language benchmark of 3,684 labeled commit transitions across
nine open-source projects, and use it to show that naive random-split evaluation
overstates predictive performance relative to a realistic, leakage-aware
leave-one-project-out protocol by a statistically significant margin (pooled ROC-AUC
0.675 vs. 0.506; bootstrap 95% CI [0.112, 0.232], p<0.0001). This finding is robust
across three algorithm families, survives a battery of statistical checks
(false-discovery-rate correction on the labeling procedure, a cluster-aware
bootstrap, a feature-ablation study isolating the underlying leakage mechanism), and
mirrors -- in a new task domain -- findings already established in cross-project
defect prediction and CI/CD build prediction, adding empirical weight to the broader
argument that cross-project software prediction tasks should, by default, be
evaluated with project-disjoint or stricter protocols rather than random splits.

I believe this manuscript fits squarely within Information and Software Technology's
stated scope of empirical studies of software engineering and management, and its
focus on methods and techniques for better engineering software development
practices. The paper's central contribution is methodological and measurement-focused
rather than the introduction of a new predictive system: it evaluates and reports
honestly on evaluation methodology itself, including several disconfirming and
negative findings we did not anticipate at the outset -- a manual audit showing only
about half of the benchmark's labeled positive class corresponds to plausible genuine
code-efficiency regressions, and a training-free heuristic baseline that fitted
machine-learning models do not yet reliably outperform under realistic evaluation. We
report these findings in full rather than presenting only favorable results, in
keeping with the empirical rigor this journal's readership expects.

This manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under
consideration elsewhere. I have no conflicts of interest to declare. All data, code,
and the benchmark dataset constructed for this study are made publicly available to
support reproducibility.

Thank you for considering this manuscript. I look forward to the reviewers' feedback.
