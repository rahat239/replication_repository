Dear Editor,

I am submitting my manuscript, "Does Naive Cross-Validation Inflate Software
Energy-Regression Prediction? A Leakage-Aware, Statistically-Gated, Cross-Project
Empirical Study," for consideration as an original research article in Expert Systems
with Applications.

Software energy consumption is an increasingly important applied concern as
organizations adopt sustainability requirements alongside functional and performance
requirements. This manuscript builds, to my knowledge, the first cross-project,
cross-language machine learning benchmark for predicting energy-regressing code
commits, and uses it to demonstrate a methodological finding with direct practical
consequences for anyone deploying intelligent systems in this space: standard
random-split evaluation protocols overstate a model's real-world predictive performance
by a statistically significant and practically meaningful margin (pooled ROC-AUC 0.675
vs. 0.506 under a realistic, leakage-aware evaluation; bootstrap 95% CI [0.112, 0.232],
p<0.0001). We show this
result is robust across three algorithm families, survives a battery of statistical
robustness checks (false-discovery-rate correction, cluster-aware bootstrapping, a
feature-ablation study isolating the leakage mechanism), and we further show that a
simple, well-established, training-free heuristic from the software engineering
literature is competitive with substantially more complex fitted models under realistic
conditions.

I believe this manuscript is well-suited to Expert Systems with Applications for three
reasons. First, it directly concerns the reliability of intelligent systems intended for
real deployment: a system that appears highly accurate in development but degrades
sharply in production is a practical failure mode of exactly the kind this journal's
readership builds and evaluates such systems to avoid. Second, the manuscript
contributes a reusable, publicly released benchmark and evaluation protocol that
future applied systems in this space can build on and be honestly measured against.
Third, in keeping with the transparency this journal values, the manuscript reports its
own negative and disconfirming findings in full -- including a manual audit that
qualifies the benchmark's label quality, and the finding that our fitted models do not
yet reliably outperform a simple heuristic baseline -- rather than presenting only
favorable results.

This manuscript is original, has not been published previously, and is not under
consideration elsewhere. I have no conflicts of interest to declare. All data, code, and
the benchmark dataset constructed for this study are made publicly available to support
reproducibility.

Thank you for considering this manuscript. I look forward to the reviewers' feedback.
